\begin{abstract}

    Manual counting of ants in behavioral studies is labor-intensive, error-prone, and impractical for large-scale research, with some studies requiring manual processing of over 13,000 images. We developed and evaluated computer vision models for automated ant detection and counting across varying colony densities and environmental conditions using three model architectures (YOLOv11n, YOLOv11m, and RT-DETR-L). Our three-study approach first determined optimal training data requirements, revealing that models trained on only 64 labeled images achieved accuracies of 73 to 88\% on simple backgrounds, such as strong contrasts between ants and their substrate, but only 51 to 63\% on complex backgrounds, such as debris and shadows that can be misclassified as ants. RT-DETR-L was found to consistently outperform YOLO variants across these conditions. Increasing the labeled images to 1,024 improved accuracy to 72-80\% on complex backgrounds. We then addressed dense colony detection (up to 1,390 ants per image) through transfer learning approaches, where fine-tuning with supervised Slicing Aided Hyper Inference (SAHI) optimization achieved 81.0\% mAP@0.5 for YOLOv11n, transforming baseline accuracy from <1\% to practical levels. Finally, we demonstrated practical applications by analyzing spatial-temporal foraging patterns, successfully detecting pathogen infection effects on ant behavior and macronutrient preferences that would be difficult to observe manually. Strong correlations between automated and manual counts ($R^2$ > 0.88) validated the approach's accuracy, though external dataset testing revealed limited generalizability across different ant species and environmental conditions. This work establishes a methodological framework for automated ant counting that enables large-scale behavioral studies while providing spatial and temporal insights beyond traditional manual methods, with complete resources publicly available to facilitate broader adoption in myrmecological research.

\end{abstract}
